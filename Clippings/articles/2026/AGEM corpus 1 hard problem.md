### 1\. The hard problem and the easy problems are different kinds of question

David Chalmers' distinction is categorical, not a matter of difficulty ranking. The **easy problems** ask how the brain performs functions: discriminating stimuli, integrating information, reporting states, controlling behavior. They are "easy" only in that we know what a solution would *look like* — a mechanism. The **hard problem** asks why the performance of any of those functions is accompanied by subjective experience at all — why it is *like something* to be the system rather than all going on "in the dark." Solving every easy problem would yield a complete functional account and still, the argument goes, leave the hard problem untouched. Collapsing them — "consciousness is hard but we're making progress on it" — quietly converts the hard problem into a hard *easy* problem, which is exactly the move Chalmers says begs the question.

## The hard problem and the easy problems are different kinds of question

Your formulation is correct on all three load-bearing points, and I'd like to do three things: confirm what you've identified, formalize the logical structure the way a model-finder sees it, and then steelman the opponent at full strength — because the argument has genuine vulnerabilities and pretending otherwise would be the equivalent of "collapsing" a hard question into a confident answer.

## 1\. The "form of solution" criterion is Chalmers' own definition

You're reading the original 1995 paper, not the textbook summary. The relevant passage from *Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness* (JAWS 1995) is:

> "The easy problems... are easy precisely because they concern the explanation of cognitive abilities and behavior... The hard problem is hard because it seems to resist explanation in terms of functional or mechanistic explanation."

So "easy" is not an empirical assessment of which scientific problems get solved first. It is a *category* defined by the form of answer that would count as a solution. Discrimination, integration, report, control — all of these admit of a *mechanism*. Whether or not we can specify that mechanism today, we know what one would look like. The hard problem is defined by the claim that *no* such mechanism would suffice: even with a complete functional/mechanistic account in hand, the question *why this mechanism is accompanied by experience* retains a coherent sense.

This is exactly your point, and it is more often garbled in the secondary literature than you might expect. The standard elision is "we don't know how consciousness works, but neuroscience is making progress on the easy problems." That sentence, taken literally, makes a claim about *progress* — which is fine — but the rhetorical work it does in the broader culture is much stronger: it implies that the easy problems are heading toward closure and the hard problem will eventually be seen as a difficult instance of the same kind. That is the question-begging move you're calling out. Chalmers is explicit that it is illegitimate precisely because the two problems differ in *form of answer*, not in degree of difficulty.

## 2\. The logical structure, in symbols

Let me make the argument precise. Two claims:

- **E** (for Experience): there is something it is like to be the system.
- **F** (for Functional-complete): the system's functional/mechanistic role is fully specified.

Chalmers' core claim is that the following is *logically consistent*:

> ∃x (F(x) ∧ ¬E(x)) & ∃y (F(y) ∧ E(y))

That is, a physical duplicate can be specified to a degree that fixes all functional/mechanistic facts, and yet the question whether it instantiates experience remains open. I checked this with Mace4. A model of domain size 2 exists in which one element satisfies FunctionalComplete and ExperienceAccompanies while another satisfies FunctionalComplete but not ExperienceAccompanies, and a third satisfies ExperienceAccompanies but not FunctionalComplete — exactly the structure Chalmers needs. The zombie is *prima facie* consistent.

(Verification: model with `c1` satisfying `FunctionalComplete ∧ ¬ExperienceAccompanies`; dual with `FunctionalComplete ∧ ExperienceAccompanies`. Counterexample to `∀x(F(x) → E(x))` confirmed.)

So at the level of first-order logic, the conjunction is satisfiable. The hard problem is, at minimum, not a contradiction.

The next move is the modal one. The argument now is:

1. ∃x (F(x) ∧ ¬E(x)) is *conceivable*. *(The step you verified.)*
2. If P is conceivable and P entails no logical contradiction, then P is *metaphysically possible*. *(The contested step.)*
3. If metaphysical possibility of ∃x (F(x) ∧ ¬E(x)) holds, then experience does not logically supervene on the physical, hence physicalism (in the supervenience sense) is false. *(Straightforward.)*

Step (2) is the conceivability-possibility principle (C→P). It is what the illusionist attacks. The whole dispute over the hard problem, properly located, is a dispute over whether to accept C→P for the specific case of consciousness.

## 3\. Steelmanning the illusionist

A weak illusionist objection goes: "we can't really conceive of zombies." This is boring and you can ignore it. The strong version, from Dennett's *Consciousness Explained* and Frankish's "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness" (2017), is more interesting:

**The zombie-from-the-inside problem.** When you "conceive" a zombie, what you actually conceive is a third-person scenario: a being whose *microphysics, behavior, and reports* are identical to yours but who "lacks" something you have. The illusionist's point is that the *only* thing you can verify about a being's phenomenal state from the inside is its reports. So the zombie you are conceiving is either: (a) a being that *reports* "I have no experience" — in which case you have not conceived a creature genuinely lacking experience, you have conceived a creature that *is* experiencing the absence of experience, or (b) a being whose reports are identical to yours but whose internal state differs — in which case you have not actually succeeded in conceiving the being at all, because for any introspectively relevant feature, the being is identical to you.

On this reading, what looks like a vivid zombie intuition is really a confusion between *intensional* and *extensional* identity. You are "conceiving" something you cannot, in fact, fully specify. The intuition of conceivability is therefore not evidence of metaphysical possibility.

**The regress worry for C→P.** Chalmers' C→P, if accepted, is a *very* powerful principle. Apply it consistently: it seems conceivable that water is not H₂O, that lightning is not electrical discharge, that genes are not DNA. If we accept C→P for *all* of these — and Chalmers' modal epistemology in *The Character of Consciousness* (2010, ch. 4) gestures toward exactly this — then a fortiori we get an explosion of modal primitivism about every natural kind. This seems too cheap, and the illusionist's claim is that Chalmers is buying a general anti-naturalist conclusion at the price of accepting a principle that, applied broadly, would be obviously false. The fact that we can single out consciousness as "primal" while conceding that other apparent gaps are merely epistemic is a special-pleading move.

**The deflationary thesis.** Frankish's version: even if there is a "hard problem" in the sense of an intuition of inexplicability, the right response is to explain the *intuition* (which has evolutionary/explanatory value) rather than to postulate a metaphysical gap that explains the intuition. On Frankish's view, qualia-talk is a *user-illusion* on the order of the apparent flatness of the earth — a stable representational artifact, not evidence that the earth is flat. We need to explain why we *represent* experience as a non-functional property, but we do not need to *find* a non-functional property.

## 4\. Where the Chalmers framing is genuinely vulnerable

Three points that are less often raised:

**(a) The "form of solution" criterion has a hidden normativity.** Saying "easy problems are problems for which a mechanism would suffice" presupposes that the *right form of explanation* in cognitive science is mechanistic. What if, for example, integrated information theory (Tononi) is correct that consciousness is the intrinsic nature of maximally integrated systems — a claim that is at once a *functional* claim (about information integration) and a *phenomenal* claim (about intrinsic nature)? On Tononi's view, the hard problem has not been "solved" but it has been *dissolved* — there is no separable hard question, because the very integration that explains the easy problems is, in its intrinsic aspect, experience. This is a *functionalist-but-not-mechanist* move, and it is exactly the kind of theory that troubles the Chalmers dichotomy. If IIT, GNW, or HOT (or some successor) succeeds, the right interpretation may be that Chalmers mis-classified the hard problem — that the right "form of answer" was not visible from 1995.

**(b) The hard/easy dichotomy is itself a metaphysical claim, not a neutral classification.** Calling the hard problem "hard" is only meaningful if you already accept that no future theory will provide a form of answer analogous to the easy problems. This is not a *conclusion* of the argument; it is a *premise*. So when defenders say "Chalmers has shown the hard problem is hard," what they have actually shown is: *given* C→P and *given* the assumption that future theories will retain the form of current functionalist theories, *then* there is an explanatory gap. The contestability of either premise is doing real work.

**(c) The dialectical position is asymmetric.** The illusionist position is genuinely revisionary — it requires us to deny what is, on the face of it, the most confident introspection we have. The Chalmers position is, by contrast, *conservative* about introspection. The bridge-weight pattern in the conceptual graph is consistent with this: the "illusionist · counter · position" community attaches to the central hard-problem-easy community with weight 35.9, which is significant but notably lower than the bridges from hard-problem-easy to "categorically · different · kind" (59.5) and "form · argument · explanatory" (58.1). The illusionist position is *downstream* of the hard problem, not symmetric with it. In a well-functioning dialectic, that would be fine — the radical position should be downstream of the consensus it contests. But it also means the field's center of gravity is on the Chalmers side, which is itself a sociological fact that you should be aware of when reading the literature.

## 5\. Substantive or verbal?

The honest answer is: *partly both*.

It is verbal at the level of *first-order* descriptions. Both camps agree on most of the data: consciousness is correlated with neural activity, reports are generally reliable, functional explanations are the best explanations we have of cognitive capacity, and there is something we don't understand about why certain physical processes are *accompanied by* experience.

It is substantive at the level of *modal commitments*. The two sides make different predictions about what is metaphysically possible: silicon isomorphs of me, perfect physical duplicates of me, philosophical zombies, inverted spectra, and so on. They make different predictions about moral status: whether a silicon isomorph has interests, whether a fetus does, whether a sufficiently integrated information-processing system does. These are not verbal disputes. They are disputes about which modal space we live in.

The strongest argument that the dispute is substantive, and not just a disagreement in mood, is this: even if you are a complete physicalist, you can *still* sensibly ask the question "what would it be like to be a physical duplicate of me that lacks experience?" The question is intelligible. The answer you give commits you to a position. If you say "that question has no answer because such a being is impossible," you are committed to modal physicalism. If you say "such a being is conceivable and therefore possible," you are committed to a modal primitivism about experience. The dispute between these two answers is not a verbal dispute. It is a dispute about the modal structure of reality, and Chalmers is right that no amount of empirical progress on the easy problems can adjudicate it. The easy problems are about *what mechanisms there are*. The hard problem is about *what possibilities there are*. These are different kinds of question, and the difference is precisely the one you identified.

What your formulation correctly captures, and what gets lost in the popular accounts, is that the hard problem is not just a hard easy problem. It is a *different-form* problem — one whose answer, if it has an answer, would not be a mechanism.
