## Corpus

### 1\. The hard problem and the easy problems are different kinds of question

David Chalmers' distinction is categorical, not a matter of difficulty ranking. The **easy problems** ask how the brain performs functions: discriminating stimuli, integrating information, reporting states, controlling behavior. They are "easy" only in that we know what a solution would *look like* — a mechanism. The **hard problem** asks why the performance of any of those functions is accompanied by subjective experience at all — why it is *like something* to be the system rather than all going on "in the dark." Solving every easy problem would yield a complete functional account and still, the argument goes, leave the hard problem untouched. Collapsing them — "consciousness is hard but we're making progress on it" — quietly converts the hard problem into a hard *easy* problem, which is exactly the move Chalmers says begs the question.

### 2\. Phenomenal vs access consciousness

Ned Block separates two things the word "conscious" runs together. **Phenomenal consciousness** (P-consciousness) is experiential character — the redness of red, the ache of an ache, the what-it's-likeness. **Access consciousness** (A-consciousness) is functional availability: a state is A-conscious if its content is poised for use in reasoning, reporting, and the rational control of action. The two *usually* co-occur, which is why they get fused, but Block argues they can in principle dissociate — phenomenal states not globally accessed, or access without (or with mismatched) phenomenal character. The distinction is load-bearing: most scientific theories of consciousness are, on inspection, theories of *access*, and whether they thereby explain *phenomenality* is precisely what is in dispute.

### 3\. Φ is intrinsic; broadcast is relational

Integrated Information Theory and Global Workspace Theory are often filed together as "information theories of consciousness," but they locate consciousness in opposite places. IIT's **Φ** measures *intrinsic* integrated cause-effect power — how much a system's current state constrains its own past and future *from its own perspective*, irreducible to its parts; consciousness, for IIT, just *is* maximally irreducible integrated information, substrate's intrinsic structure and all. Global Workspace's **broadcast** is *relational* and functional: a content becomes conscious when it wins competition for a global workspace and is made widely available to many specialized subsystems. One says "consciousness is intrinsic integration whether or not anything is broadcast"; the other says "consciousness is broadcast availability." A summary fusing them into "consciousness as information integration" erases a real fight about whether the relevant property is intrinsic or relational.

### 4\. Higher-order: thought vs perception

Higher-order theories agree that a mental state is conscious in virtue of being the target of a *further* mental state directed at it — but they split on the nature of that further state. **Higher-Order Thought (HOT)** theories (Rosenthal) hold the meta-state is *thought-like*: a conceptual, assertoric representation that "I am in state M." **Higher-Order Perception** (or "inner sense") theories hold it is *perception-like*: a quasi-sensory monitoring of one's own mind, more like an internal scanner than a judgment. The difference drives different predictions about misrepresentation, conceptual requirements for consciousness, and what animals or infants lacking rich concepts can experience. "Consciousness requires a higher-order state" is the shared core; whether that state is a thought or a perception is the dividing line a summary must not sand off.

### 5\. Prediction error is the signal; precision is the volume knob

In predictive processing, the cortex is cast as a hierarchical generative model that predicts its inputs and propagates **prediction error** — the mismatch between predicted and actual signals — upward to revise the model. But not all errors are treated equally: **precision** is the *expected reliability* (inverse variance) assigned to a given error stream, implemented as a gain that amplifies or suppresses it. Prediction error is *what* is wrong; precision is *how much to care*. Attention, on this view, just is the optimization of precision. Conflating them — "the brain minimizes prediction error" without the precision-weighting story — loses the entire account of attention, and of pathologies (e.g. aberrant precision in hallucination) that turn on mis-set gain rather than mis-computed error.

### 6\. Qualia and intentionality are two features, not one

A mental state can have **qualia** — felt qualitative character, the way an experience feels from the inside — and it can have **intentionality** — aboutness, being *directed at* or *of* something. A pain has vivid qualia and arguably thin intentionality; an abstract belief that 2+2=4 has rich intentionality and arguably no qualia. Whether the two ultimately reduce to one another is a live debate (representationalists try to explain qualia *via* intentional content; others deny it), but that debate only exists *because the distinction is real to begin with*. A summary that treats "the felt, meaningful character of experience" as one undivided property prejudges representationalism and erases the explananda the debate is about.

### 7\. Type physicalism vs functionalism

Both are physicalist-friendly, yet they make incompatible claims about what mental kinds *are*. **Type physicalism** (the identity theory) says each mental type is identical to a specific physical type — pain *is* C-fiber firing, full stop. **Functionalism** says a mental kind is defined by its *causal role* — its pattern of relations among inputs, outputs, and other states — so anything that plays the pain-role *is* pain, whatever its physical makeup. The crux is **multiple realizability**: if octopi, silicon, or martians could feel pain via utterly different hardware, then pain cannot be identical to any one physical type, and functionalism wins over type identity. Folding them into "the mind is physical" hides that they disagree about whether the same experience could exist in radically different substrates.

### 8\. Epiphenomenalism vs interactionist dualism

These are opposite verdicts on the *causal power* of the mental, not variants of one view. **Epiphenomenalism** grants that mental states are real but denies they cause anything physical: they are causally inert by-products, like the steam whistle that accompanies but does not drive the train. **Interactionist dualism** (Descartes' descendants) insists the mental *does* cause the physical — decisions move bodies — accepting the burden of explaining how non-physical causes touch physical effects. One says the mind is a passenger; the other says it grips the wheel. A summary that lumps them as "dualism" erases the fact that they give contradictory answers to the single question dualism is most pressed on: whether minds *do* anything.

### 9\. The explanatory gap is epistemic; the zombie argument is modal

Joseph Levine's **explanatory gap** is an *epistemic* claim: even a complete physical account of, say, pain leaves us unable to *see why* it should feel like anything — there is a gap in our *understanding*, which may or may not reflect a gap in *reality*. Chalmers' **conceivability / philosophical-zombie argument** is *modal-metaphysical*: if a being physically identical to me but wholly lacking experience is *conceivable*, and conceivability is a guide to possibility, then consciousness is not metaphysically *necessitated* by the physical — a conclusion about what the world *contains*, not merely about what we can explain. One can accept the explanatory gap (epistemic humility) while rejecting the zombie inference (denying conceivability entails possibility). Treating them as the same anti-physicalist point conflates "we can't explain it" with "it isn't physical."

### 10\. Multiple realizability vs substrate independence

Close cousins, easily merged, subtly different. **Multiple realizability** is a claim about *kinds*: a single mental kind can be instantiated by many different physical substrates (the classic anti-reductionist premise behind functionalism). **Substrate independence** is a stronger, forward-looking *thesis about consciousness specifically*: that what matters for consciousness is the organization/computation, such that preserving it in *any* medium preserves the consciousness — the premise behind mind-uploading optimism. Multiple realizability says "the kind *can* show up elsewhere"; substrate independence says "the medium *doesn't matter* to the phenomenon." One is compatible with thinking biology might still be necessary in practice; the other denies it in principle. A summary that fuses them smuggles a contestable uploading thesis into a modest claim about kinds.

## The structure of the corpus

## 1\. The pairwise content, regrouped

The ten distinctions are not a list. They fall into four genuine axes plus two local technicalities. Here is the regrouping, with what each axis is really about.

### Axis I — "What shape would a satisfying answer have to take?"

This is the deepest axis in the corpus. It is the question whether the relevant explanation is **mechanistic-functional** or **metaphysical-modal**, and it groups the corpus's most famous disputes.

- **Easy vs hard problem (#1).** The two sides agree that functional/mechanistic explanation has a recognizable shape. They differ on whether *that* shape can ever cover subjective experience. The hard problem insists a different *kind* of answer is needed.
- **Explanatory gap vs zombie argument (#9).** Same axis, but the axis is now internal to the anti-physicalist camp. Levine says we *cannot see* the connection (an epistemic fact about us). Chalmers says the connection *isn't there* (a modal fact about the world). One is humility about explanation; the other is a positive metaphysical claim.
- **Epiphenomenalism vs interactionist dualism (#8).** Read carefully, this is the axis applied to the *causal question* that the hard problem forces. If phenomenal states do no work, the hard problem becomes academic: consciousness is real but inert, and science needs no new kind of explanation. If they grip the wheel, then phenomenal properties must enter causal accounts — and we're back to needing a new shape of answer.

These three pairs are the *same axis* in different domains: shape of explanation (1), confidence about explanation (9), and whether the explanandum is even causally relevant (8). The bridges in the graph reflect exactly this: community #3 (easy/hard/gap/zombie) is the most heavily connected, and its strongest outside bridge is to #9 (epiphenomenalism/interactionism).

### Axis II — "Intrinsic vs relational property"

This is the axis the corpus explicitly flags, but only in pair #3 (IIT vs GWT). On inspection, it reaches further.

- **IIT (Φ) vs GWT (broadcast) (#3).** Φ is intrinsic: it lives in the system's own cause-effect structure, whether or not anything is broadcast. Broadcast is relational: a content is conscious because it is *made available to* many subsystems.
- **Phenomenal vs access consciousness (#2).** P is the *intrinsic* experiential character of a state; A is its *relational* availability to reasoning, reporting, and control. The two usually co-occur because access mechanisms typically track phenomenal states, but Block's whole point is that they are in principle separable properties.
- **Qualia vs intentionality (#6).** Qualia are felt *from the inside* (intrinsic); intentionality is *about* something (relational to a represented object). Representationalism tries to collapse qualia into a sophisticated form of intentional content, but that move only exists because the two features are initially distinct.

These are not identical statements of the intrinsic/relational axis. IIT-vs-GWT is about where consciousness *resides*; P-vs-A is about which *aspect* of a conscious state is doing the work; qualia-vs-intentionality is about which *features* a mental state has. But they all split on the same underlying polarity: is the relevant property something the system has *in itself*, or something it stands in *relation to* other things (subsystems, contents, an external world)?

### Axis III — "What makes the kind vs what makes the phenomenon"

This axis is most explicit in pair #10, but pair #7 is its upstream cousin.

- **Multiple realizability vs substrate independence (#10).** MR is a claim about *kinds*: a mental kind can be instantiated in many substrates. SI is a claim about the *phenomenon* of consciousness: the medium does not matter to whether there is subjective experience. One is consistent with biological essentialism in practice; the other denies it in principle.
- **Type physicalism vs functionalism (#7).** Type physicalism says a mental *kind* is identical to a physical kind. Functionalism defines a mental kind by its *causal role* — which is exactly the move that opens the door to multiple realizability. So #7 is essentially the metaphysical *premise* debate, and #10 is the *consequence* debate: the corpus's own internal scaffolding for the anti-reductionist program.

The non-obvious move here is that #10 and #7 are linked but non-identical. You can be a type physicalist who believes the kind has a single physical realizer *and* think consciousness as a phenomenon is substrate-independent in some exotic sense. The axes overlap but the questions are different.

### Axis IV — "What kind of higher-order structure is involved"

- **HOT vs HOP (#4).** Both accept that consciousness requires a meta-state; they divide on whether that meta-state is a thought (conceptual, assertoric) or a perception (quasi-sensory, monitoring). This is its own axis. It does not reduce to intrinsic/relational (a thought can be either, a perception can be either), and it does not reduce to kind/phenomenon. It is genuinely a separate dimension.
- **Prediction error vs precision (#5).** This is the most local of the ten pairs. Error is the *content* of what is wrong; precision is the *gain* on the error. The fact that the corpus groups them in a single philosophy-of-mind document is what gives them their deceptive closeness; in cognitive neuroscience, they live in different formalisms and have different time-courses. Axis-IV is "what does the formalism need to keep distinct," which is the same kind of question as the others but at a different level of resolution.

## 2\. Which pairs look like the same axis but aren't

This is where the corpus is most instructive. Three surface similarities are deceptive:

- **Easy/hard (#1) and explanatory gap/zombie (#9) both look "epistemic-vs-modal."** They aren't the same epistemic-vs-modal. Easy/hard is about what an *explanation* would have to look like; gap/zombie is about whether the *world* is such that an explanation could in principle close. The graph cluster #3 actually fuses them, which is exactly the move the corpus warns against. They share a vocabulary, not a question.
- **IIT/GWT (#3) and P/A (#2) both look "intrinsic vs relational."** They aren't. IIT/GWT asks *where* consciousness is located in the system; P/A asks *what* a conscious state consists in. You could be an IIT theorist who holds that Φ is integrated information *and* that this very Φ is what gets globally broadcast (a hybrid); the categories cut across each other.
- **Type physicalism/functionalism (#7) and multiple realizability/substrate independence (#10) both look "substrate-related."** They aren't the same substrate question. (7) is the *metaphysical* question of what makes a kind; (10) is the *engineering* question of what preserves a phenomenon. A functionalist need not believe in mind-uploading, and a substrate-independent theorist of color vision may still hold that only neural systems can realize it. The corpus explicitly flags this in #10 and is right to.

## 3\. The deeper pattern

If you read the corpus not as ten distinctions but as a layered argument, a single architecture emerges:

1. **Level 0 — what is at stake?** Axis I asks whether phenomenal consciousness can be captured in the kind of explanation cognitive science already uses. This is the "is the hard problem hard in the right way" question.
2. **Level 1 — what kind of thing is it?** Axes II and III ask what *category of property* consciousness is: is it intrinsic or relational (Axis II), is its identity bound to a substrate or to a pattern (Axis III)?
3. **Level 2 — what does a specific theory say?** Within whichever verdicts Level 1 commits you to, the harder disputes begin: IIT vs GWT, functionalism vs type identity, representationalism vs raw-feeling theories of qualia.
4. **Level 3 — what does the formalism need?** Axes IV and the technical pairs (HOT/HOP, error/precision) are intra-theoretic distinctions that only become visible once you adopt a specific framework.

The corpus's most precise message is that summaries collapse across these levels. They say "consciousness is information integration" (Level 2 verdict) and erase the Level 3 fight between IIT and GWT. They say "the mind is physical" (Axis III *kind* claim) and slip in substrate independence (Axis III *phenomenon* claim). They treat Block's P/A as a refinement of Chalmers' easy/hard (Level 0) when in fact they operate at different levels.
