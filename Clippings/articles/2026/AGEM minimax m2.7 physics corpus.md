## Copenhagen

The wavefunction is not a physical object inhabiting the world; it is a computational device that encodes the probabilities of measurement outcomes. Before measurement, a quantum system has no definite value for an observable — the question of where the particle "really is" between observations is meaningless. Measurement is special: it triggers the collapse of the wavefunction from a superposition into a single definite state, and this collapse is real, irreversible, and lies outside the unitary Schrödinger evolution. There is a fundamental cut between the quantum system and the classical measuring apparatus, which must be described in ordinary classical language. Complementary properties such as position and momentum cannot be simultaneously well-defined; the experimental arrangement determines which aspect of reality manifests. The observer's choice of what to measure is therefore constitutive of the outcome. Probability here is irreducible and objective: nature itself is indeterministic, and the state vector exhausts what can be said about a system.

### Where Copenhagen Is Strongest

**1\. Operational Success.** Copenhagen encodes the operational practice of quantum mechanics without assuming extra structure. For working physicists, it says: *shut up and calculate, but know why you can't ask certain questions*. This pragmatic orientation is not a weakness — it is a discipline.

**2\. The Irreversibility Insight.** Collapse as genuinely irreversible — outside Schrödinger evolution — correctly captures the thermodynamically asymmetric arrow of measurement. Macroscopic records are not overwritten; this is why measurement is special.

**3\. Complementarity as a Constraint.** Bohr's insight that experimental context determines which properties manifest remains philosophically potent. Nature does not have a single story; it has many stories, mutually exclusive but jointly exhaustive.

**4\. Irreducible Probability.** If Copenhagen is right, nature is not merely epistemically unknowable — it is *ontologically chancy*. This is a metaphysically serious position (closer to Popper than to Laplacian determinism).

### Where Copenhagen Is Vulnerable

**1\. The Quantum-Classical Boundary (The Fundamental Cut).**

This is Copenhagen's Achilles heel. Where *exactly* does the quantum end and the classical begin? Copenhagen gives no principled answer — only a gesture toward "large" or "irreversible" or "classical." The problem:

- If the cut is placed at the measuring apparatus, the apparatus itself must be treated quantum-mechanically in principle. But then its quantum nature is admitted, and the cut becomes arbitrary.
- If consciousness is the cut (as von Neumann vaguely suggested), this elevates minds to a privileged ontic status with no independent justification.
- Decoherence provides a *mechanism* for apparent classicality but does **not** solve the measurement problem — it explains why interference terms vanish, not why a *single outcome* is realized. Copenhagen needs collapse, but decoherence only delays it.

**Consequence:** The fundamental cut is either arbitrary (bad metaphysics) or dualistic (worse metaphysics).

**2\. The Measurement Problem Persists.**

Copenhagen declares collapse real but offers no dynamical mechanism. Schrödinger evolution is universally valid — except when it isn't. *When, exactly, does non-unitary evolution kick in?* The theory provides no cutoff, no rule, no derivation. Copenhagen thus stands as an *instrumentalist description* of a process it refuses to explain.

**3\. Wigner's Friend and the Observer's Status.**

Extended Wigner's-friend scenarios (measured by a friend inside a lab, then measured by Wigner outside) create a tension: does the friend count as an observer with collapse-activating status? Copenhagen's answer is unclear:

- If the friend collapses the wavefunction, then Wigner's measurement of the friend's lab should yield a definite result — but then entanglement between Wigner and friend+system must be acknowledged pre-measurement, and the cut is in Wigner's head, not in the friend's lab.
- If the friend does *not* collapse, then we get many-worlds-like branching inside the lab, and Copenhagen collapses into Everett.

**4\. The Wavefunction's Status.**

Copenhagen says the wavefunction is a "computational device" — not a physical object. This is a bold epistemic thesis. But:

- If the wavefunction represents *physical reality* (even partially), it has the right to an ontic reading.
- If it is purely epistemic (as in QBism, where it encodes individual agent beliefs), then collapse is a *belief-update*, not a physical event — and Copenhagen collapses into a subjectivist variant.

The computational-device language is stable between these readings, but they have very different implications for what collapse *is*.

### Where Many-Worlds Is Vulnerable

**1\. The Preferred Basis Problem.**

This is the most fundamental objection and deserves careful treatment. The wavefunction lives in Hilbert space; branches live in *configuration space*. But Hilbert space has a vast preferred structure, and the question is: **what selects the preferred basis in which branching occurs?**

Decoherence helps but doesn't fully solve this:

- Decoherence explains *why* certain bases become stable and classical — pointer states are robust under environmental entanglement (position, momentum, etc.).
- But decoherence does not explain *why* one set of pointer states is realized and not another. The reduced density matrix is still a proper mixture, not an improper one. It does not pick out a unique decomposition.

The problem is that the "branching structure" is basis-dependent. In any non-degenerate Hilbert space, there are infinitely many ways to decompose the state into "branches." MWI needs a principled argument that one decomposition is *physically real* and the others are not.

**The Saunders-Wallace-Zurek response** (multiverse from quantum logic, decision-theoretic probability) is the most sophisticated attempt, but it is not yet universally accepted as definitive.

**2\. The Probability Problem.**

This is MWI's deepest wound. If all outcomes occur, why should I expect the Born rule statistics? MWI cannot appeal to counting branches — naive counting of equally real branches gives the wrong answer (e.g., two branches from a 50/50 spin measurement would predict 50/50, but the amplitudes differ in general).

The options are:

**(a) Branching counting with amplitude weighting.** Everett's original proposal: the *measure* of a branch is proportional to the squared modulus of its amplitude. But this is circular — it just asserts the Born rule.

**(b) Decision-theoretic approaches** (Deutsch, Saunders, Wallace): show that any rational agent who believes MWI must act as if Born-rule probabilities govern their decisions. The argument: given that you exist in all branches, but your subjective experience of "finding yourself" in a branch is weighted by branch measure, rationality forces you to assign subjective probabilities matching the Born rule.

**(c) Envariance** (Zurek): quantum environment-assisted invariance. Given entanglement with the environment, the only invariant way to count branches is by squared amplitudes — because swapping amplitudes that are equal in magnitude leaves the global state unchanged.

The decision-theoretic program is the most developed and has genuine force, but critics argue it smuggles in normative assumptions (what counts as a rational decision) that are themselves interpretations.

**Critical point:** Copenhagen *has* irreducible probability. MWI must *derive* it. Until the derivation is uncontroversial, MWI is incomplete as a probability theory.

**3\. The Measure Problem and Branch Counting.**

Even granting decoherence and branching, there is the **measure problem**: what is the correct measure over branches? If branches have equal ontological status, the measure is arbitrary. If the measure is amplitude-squared, why? MWI owes us an account of why some branches are "more real" or "more numerous" in the relevant sense — not merely "have larger amplitude."

This is structurally similar to the measure problem in cosmology (why are we in a typical observer?), and the analogy is not reassuring.

**4\. The Experience Problem.**

MWI claims definite experience is a "parochial slice." But this raises hard questions:

- If all branches are equally real, and I experience branch A, what is the relationship between *me* in branch A and *me* in branch B?
- Is there a further fact about which branch is "indexed to" this experience?
- Does MWI require a *second* layer of ontology — a "centered" observer that tracks across branches — or is this just Copenhagen's observer in disguise?

The parochial slice thesis is intuitively compelling but philosophically underdeveloped. It needs a theory of personal identity across branching events.

**5\. Testability and Emptiness.**

MWI and Copenhagen make the same empirical predictions. This is usually presented as a virtue (both reproduce QM), but it is also a problem: **a theory that makes no novel empirical predictions is difficult to falsify.** If MWI cannot be distinguished from Copenhagen by any experiment, it risks being unfalsifiable in the strong sense. This is not fatal (string theory faces the same objection), but it means MWI is currently more a philosophical preference than a empirically grounded theory.

**6\. Vague Self-Location.**

The claim that probability arises from "self-location uncertainty" is philosophically attractive but technically thin. The Everettian needs to explain:

- What is the sample space for self-location? (The set of all branches.)
- What is the probability measure? (Amplitude-squared, or derived.)
- Why should rational agents have credence proportional to branch measure? (Decision-theoretic argument required.)

Without the decision-theoretic or envariant derivation, self-location uncertainty is just a name for the Born rule problem, not a solution.

## Bohmian Mechanics

Particles always have definite positions, at every instant, whether or not anyone is measuring them. The wavefunction is real, but it is not the whole story: it is a guiding field that determines, through the guidance equation, how the configuration of particles evolves. The theory is fully deterministic — given the initial positions and the wavefunction, the entire future trajectory is fixed. There is no collapse and no special role for the observer or for measurement; a measurement is just an ordinary physical interaction governed by the same laws as everything else. The apparent randomness of quantum outcomes reflects our ignorance of the actual initial positions, distributed according to the squared amplitude of the wavefunction. The cost is explicit nonlocality: the guiding field depends instantaneously on the configuration of all particles, however far apart. Superposition is a property of the wavefunction, never of the particles themselves, which are always somewhere definite.

### Where Bohmian Mechanics Is Strongest

**1\. Particle Ontology and Common-Sense Intuition.**

This is Bohmian mechanics' most seductive feature. Particles have definite positions at all times — the world is made of things in places, moving along trajectories. This is classical-like realism without classical mechanics' failures. For philosophers of science weary of quantum strangeness, this feels like coming home.

**2\. Determinism Without Collapse.**

Bohmian mechanics is the only mainstream interpretation that delivers both determinism and empirical adequacy without invoking many worlds. The entire history of the universe — past, present, and future — is encoded in the initial wavefunction and particle positions. There is no genuine probability, only epistemic ignorance. This is a deeply satisfying metaphysical picture.

**3\. No Arbitrary Quantum-Classical Cut.**

Like MWI, Bohmian mechanics eliminates the measurement postulate. A "measurement" is just an interaction that entangles the system with the apparatus, and the particle's trajectory through the apparatus determines the outcome. The pointer's position is determined by the particle's pre-existing position. No special process, no collapse.

**4\. Explicit Dynamics for Measurement.**

Unlike Copenhagen (which postulates collapse) and MWI (which dissolves measurement into branching), Bohmian mechanics provides an *actual dynamical story* of what happens during a measurement. The particle enters a region of the wavefunction, the guidance equation steers it, and the pointer moves. This is explanatory in a way that Copenhagen's postulate is not.

**5\. The Guidance Equation as Explanatory Structure.**

The guidance equation doesn't just *describe* outcomes — it *explains* them. The wavefunction's structure determines the particle's trajectory. Quantum phenomena like the double-slit interference pattern, the Aharonov-Bohm effect, and quantum tunneling all receive clean dynamical explanations. Bohmian mechanics is not merely empirically equivalent — it is, in a specific sense, *more explanatory* than the operational approach.

### Where Bohmian Mechanics Is Vulnerable

**1\. Nonlocality.**

This is the most serious cost, and it is severe. The guidance equation is:

$$\\frac{d\\mathbf{Q}}{dt} = \\frac{\\hbar}{m}\\Im \\frac{\\nabla \\psi}{\\psi}$$

The velocity of *any* particle at any instant depends on the wavefunction evaluated at *all* particle positions — including particles arbitrarily distant. The theory is explicitly, structurally nonlocal.

Bell's theorem shows that *any* local hidden-variable theory must violate the Bell inequalities, and quantum mechanics violates them too. But Bohmian mechanics violates them *while remaining deterministic* — and the violation is nonlocal. This is not a surprising or accidental feature; it is built into the theory's architecture.

**Is nonlocality fatal?**

- **No, if you are a realist who accepts that nature is nonlocal.** EPR-Bohm experiments show that nonlocal correlations exist. Bohmian mechanics provides a clear mechanism.
- **Yes, if you are a localist.** Many philosophers and physicists (including Bell himself) found the nonlocality uncomfortable but unavoidable if you want both realism and determinism.

The nonlocality in Bohmian mechanics is also \* мир\*a *passive* nonlocality — the wavefunction carries influence, but no signal can be sent superluminally. This is a relief (no causal paradoxes), but it means the nonlocality is epistemically inaccessible in the usual sense.

**2\. The Quantum Equilibrium Hypothesis.**

This is Bohmian mechanics' analog of MWI's probability problem. The theory must specify *why* particle positions are distributed according to $|\\psi|^2$ — the quantum equilibrium distribution. This is required to reproduce Born rule statistics, but:

- The equilibrium hypothesis is *additional* to the fundamental dynamics.
- It is not *derived* from the dynamics — it is postulated.
- The theory needs a justification for why systems are typically in equilibrium.

Dürr, Goldstein, and Zanghì have argued that equilibrium follows from typicality — if initial conditions are typical (with respect to the equilibrium measure), then equilibrium is generic. But "typicality" is a measure choice that is itself an additional postulate, just like MWI's branch measure.

**The situation mirrors MWI:** Bohmian mechanics must explain probability (or typicality), and the explanation requires structure not present in the fundamental equations. It is arguably *easier* than MWI's probability problem (since Bohmian mechanics has a natural measure on configuration space), but it is not fully resolved.

**3\. Spin and Relativistic Generalization.**

Bohmian mechanics was first formulated for spinless particles in nonrelativistic quantum mechanics. Spin introduces complications:

- Particles with spin cannot be described by point particles in 3D configuration space — the wavefunction has internal degrees of freedom that don't map naturally onto particle positions.
- The relativistic generalization is genuinely difficult. Dirac particles require a different formulation; quantum field theory requires either particle creation/annihilation (which sits uneasily with particle trajectories) or a field ontology (which loses the particle interpretation's appeal).

Several programs exist (Bohmian quantum field theory, Diracsea approaches, algebraic approaches), but none is yet fully satisfying. This is a genuine lacuna.

**4\. The Measurement-of-Particle-Positions Problem.**

Bohmian mechanics says particles always have definite positions. But we don't directly observe particle positions in most experiments — we observe pointer positions, flash trails, detector clicks. The theory must explain why *we* (the macroscopic apparatus + observers) register definite outcomes.

The explanation: our bodies, the apparatus, and the pointer are all collections of particles with definite positions. The wavefunction guides them, and their trajectories determine the pointer position. This works — but it means "measurement" in Bohmian mechanics is always measurement of *particle positions*. What if we want to measure something else (spin, say)? The story becomes more complex.

**5\. Empirical Underdetermination.**

Bohmian mechanics makes exactly the same empirical predictions as standard quantum mechanics. There is no experiment, even in principle, that distinguishes it from Copenhagen or MWI. This is simultaneously its virtue (empirically adequate) and its vice (unfalsifiable relative to QM).

### Where QBism Is Most Vulnerable

**1\. The Incommunicability Problem.**

If quantum states are personal, how do agents *communicate* about outcomes? When Alice measures a spin and gets "up," and Bob measures the same system, do they necessarily agree?

QBism's answer is that they *do* agree on outcomes because outcomes are **public** in a specific sense: they are consequences experienced by agents in the same local community. But this raises a harder question: *what guarantees intersubjective agreement?*

Copenhagen postulates a public world of classical apparatus. MWI has all agents in all branches. Bohmian has all particles in definite positions. QBism has only personal experience — and the question of why my experiences track yours (except by assumption) is not answered.

The incommunicability problem is the *inverse* of Wigner's friend: MWI struggles with *too much* agreement (all branches are equally real); QBism struggles with *too little* (all states are personal).

**2\. The Miracle of Coherence.**

QBism must explain why subjective beliefs track long-run frequencies — why, if I bet according to the Born rule, I tend not to lose money over many trials. This is the **reflection principle** problem: why should an agent's beliefs about future experiences be calibrated by past frequencies?

Bayesian coherence arguments can show that rational agents *should* track frequencies, but this requires a further argument that the world has stable regularities that reward calibrated beliefs. QBism has no *physical* story for why this is true — it has a *normative* story for why agents should act as if it is true.

The "miracle of coherence" (to borrow van Fraassen's phrase about frequentism) remains acute. If the world were chaotic, QBism would be useless — but it isn't. QBism doesn't explain why the world is quantum-mechanically regular.

**3\. Solipsism and the External World.**

QBism is not solipsism, but it is vulnerable to the charge. The formalism makes no claims about an external reality; it only describes an agent's experiences. QBists reply that this is *epistemologically humble* rather than solipsistic — QBism doesn't *deny* an external world, it just says QM doesn't describe it.

But this response is difficult to sustain. If QM is a "user's manual" and the user doesn't appear in the manual, then the manual's existence is unexplained. QBism requires a prior commitment to an external world (to ground the coherence of experiences), but this commitment is not derivable from the formalism.

**4\. The Heisenberg Cut Shifted.**

In Copenhagen, the Heisenberg cut is a physical boundary: here quantum, here classical. In QBism, the cut is between the agent's *personal experience* and everything else. This is not necessarily a fatal objection, but it shifts the cut from physics to consciousness studies without solving the underlying problem.

**5\. No Account of What the Formalism Tracks.**

QBism refuses to say what the wavefunction *represents* in the world. It is a user's manual, but for what? The QBist answer — "for the consequences of your actions on the world" — presupposes a world to act upon but says nothing about what that world is like in itself.

The Dutch-book argument establishes that *if* you assign quantum states, *then* you must update by the Born rule to avoid incoherence. ## Relational Quantum Mechanics

There is no absolute, observer-independent state of a system. A quantum state is always relative to a particular observer or physical system with which the system in question has interacted. Values of observables are not intrinsic properties but are actualized only in the interaction between two systems, and they hold only relative to that second system. The observer carries no special status and need not be conscious or classical; any physical system serves equally well as the system relative to which states are defined. The wavefunction does not describe the world absolutely; it describes how one system would find another upon interacting.

### Where RQM Is Strongest

**1\. Solving the Measurement Problem Without Metaphysical Excess.**

RQM's response to the measurement problem is elegant: there is no measurement problem because there is no absolute state to collapse. When system *A* interacts with system *B*, *A* 's state relative to *B* becomes definite. This is not "collapse" in the physical sense — it is the *actualization of a relation*. There is no "system going from superposition to definite" because the superposition was never absolute in the first place.

This dissolves the measurement problem at its root: the puzzle was caused by assuming that quantum states describe absolute properties. Once that assumption is dropped, the problem disappears.

**2\. No Quantum-Classical Cut.**

In Copenhagen, the cut is between quantum and classical. In RQM, *every* physical system is a potential observer, and every interaction is a potential measurement. There is no privileged class of "classical" systems. The cut is dissolved, not relocated.

This is similar to MWI and Bohmian in eliminating the cut — but RQM's reason is different: not because everything is quantum (MWI) or because particles have definite positions (Bohmian), but because quantum states are always relative to some system, and the question of which system is "really classical" is illegitimate.

**3\. Objectivity Without Absoluteness.**

RQM offers a genuine third way between objectivism and subjectivism. The moon is not "really there" in an absolute sense — but it is there *relative to you*, *relative to the Earth*, *relative to every physical system with which it has interacted*. Its relational properties are as objective as anything in physics.

This is not solipsism because:

- Other systems have real states (relative to their own observers)
- Intersubjective agreement arises from interactions: when two systems *A* and *B* interact, they establish a shared relation, and both agree on the outcome of that interaction
- The world is real; only absolute states are denied

**4\. Information-Theoretic Foundation Without Subjectivism.**

RQM is often presented as an information-theoretic interpretation: quantum mechanics is about the information that physical systems have about each other. But unlike QBism, this information is *physical* (encoded in the relational states of physical systems) rather than *personal* (encoded in an agent's beliefs).

This gives RQM a realist foundation while keeping information central — potentially bridging quantum foundations with quantum information theory in a way that QBism cannot.

**5\. Elegant Resolution of Wigner's Friend.**

RQM resolves Wigner's friend cleanly: the friend and the system have established a relation, so the friend's state relative to the system is definite. Wigner's state relative to the friend is also definite after they interact. There is no paradox — only a nested structure of relations. Both descriptions are correct, each in its own relational frame.

### Where RQM Is Most Vulnerable

**1\. The Coordination Problem.**

This is the deepest objection and deserves careful treatment. If all states are relational, how do different relational frames *agree* on outcomes when they interact?

RQM's answer: when two systems *A* and *B* interact, they become correlated. After the interaction:

- *A* 's state relative to *B* encodes information about *B*
- *B* 's state relative to *A* encodes information about *A*
- Both "know" that the interaction occurred and agree on its outcome

This is the RQM analog of "measurement." The two systems now share a common relation.

But the harder question: *why* do these shared relations track external facts? RQM says the world is a network of relations, and these relations are the facts. But what grounds the *stability* of this network? Decoherence provides an answer (environmental interactions suppress interference between different relational states), but decoherence is a physical process in RQM — and whether decoherence is fundamental or emergent is left open.

**2\. The Missing World Problem.**

If all states are relational, what is the "world" made of? RQM's answer: the world is the totality of relations. But this answer has a haunting quality — there are no things, only relations between things.

Critics charge that this is a form of **idealism** (Berkeley without God): properties exist only in relations, and relations are not things, so nothing ultimately exists. RQM's defenders reply that this is not idealism because:

- Relations are real (they are physical states)
- The network of relations is mind-independent
- The "missing world" is a conceptual error: there is no world over and above the relations

The debate turns on whether "realism about relations" is coherent without "realism about relata." The philosophical jury is still out.

**3\. The Identity of Indiscernibles Problem.**

If all physical systems are equivalent as "observers," what distinguishes one system from another? RQM needs a principled account of why there are many distinct systems rather than one universal system.

The formalism of QM treats systems as subsystems of a larger Hilbert space — but this formalism is imported from standard QM, not derived from RQM's relational principles. RQM owes us a story about why the world decomposes into discrete systems in the way it does.

**4\. The Problem of Common Environments.**

Decoherence in RQM is the process by which systems become correlated with their environments. But if all states are relational, the "environment" is just another set of relational states. The question is: why do certain relational frames (our macroscopic one) give stable, approximately classical descriptions?

RQM can appeal to decoherence — but decoherence in RQM is a relational process, not an absolute one. It explains why certain relations are stable without explaining why *these particular* relational frames (human-scale systems, classical instruments) are the stable ones.

**5\. The Born Rule in RQM.**

RQM derives the Born rule from the informational structure of quantum mechanics: given a system with possible outcomes, an observer should assign probabilities matching the squared amplitudes. But RQM does not have a fully satisfying *normative* story for why these probabilities should govern an agent's bets.

RQM's approach is closer to QBism's Dutch-book argument than to MWI's decision-theoretic program — but RQM must also explain why the informational structure of physics yields *these* probabilities rather than others. The derivation is not yet complete.
