# Mental Imagery

## Definition

Mental imagery is the cognitive capacity to form internal representations of absent or hypothetical objects, scenes, or events — seeing something in the "mind's eye" without physical sensory input. It encompasses visual imagery (the most studied form), as well as auditory, tactile, and other sensory modalities. The key property is that imagery is **depictive** rather than **propositional** — it preserves the spatial and perceptual structure of what it represents, similar to actual perception.

## Classical Taxonomy

### Phantasia vs Hyperaphantasia

Individual differences in imagery vividness form a spectrum:

- **Phantasia** (low vividness): Reduced or absent visual imagery; aphantasia = complete absence. ~2-4% of population has aphantasia — they cannot form voluntary visual imagery.
- **Hyperaphantasia** (high vividness): Exceptionally vivid imagery that can approach perceptual intensity. Less studied but increasingly recognized.

Most people fall between these extremes, and vividness is domain-specific (someone may have vivid visual imagery but weak auditory imagery).

### Relationship to Perception

Mental imagery shares neural substrate with perception — visual imagery activates early visual cortex (V1-V3) even in the absence of visual input. This is why imagery can be interrupted by visual tasks (imagery and perception compete for shared neural resources). The **reverse hierarchy** principle explains this: high-level representations (in frontal cortex) can cascade down to drive low-level sensory areas.

## Neural Mechanisms

### Frontoparietal Attention Network

Mental imagery depends on a frontoparietal attention network — particularly the **superior frontal gyrus** (attention to internally generated content) and **intraparietal sulcus** (maintaining and manipulating visual representations). Damage to these areas (e.g., in parietal neglect) can selectively impair imagery while sparing perception.

### Top-Down Generation vs Bottom-Up Retrieval

Imagery is not a simple "memory replay" — it requires active generation of visual content from semantic descriptions. This is why imagery can be novel (you can imagine a purple elephant you've never seen) and why it can be combined creatively (imagining a scene that never existed). The generation process uses the same high-level visual processing pathways (inferotemporal cortex) as perception.

### V1 Activation Question

The degree to which early visual cortex (V1) is activated during imagery remains debated:
- Some fMRI studies show strong V1 involvement during vivid imagery
- Others show V1 is not reliably activated unless imagery involves low-level spatial attention
- This may reflect individual differences and task demands rather than a single answer

## AI Connection: Latent Space Imagery

In AI systems, "imagery" operates differently:
- **Latent space traversal**: Moving through the model's internal representation space to generate novel outputs; akin to imagery because it produces novel configurations without direct external prompting
- **Counterfactual generation**: Producing "what if" scenarios via latent space interpolation
- **Mental simulation**: Using world-models to simulate outcomes without environmental interaction

The connection to human imagery is analogical — both involve generating internally-driven representations rather than externally-driven perception. The key distinction: human imagery draws on embodied perceptual experience; AI imagery (in current systems) draws on statistical regularities in training data.

## Connection to Imagination

[[imagination]] is the broader cognitive capacity; mental imagery is a specific modality of imaginative process. Imagery provides the representational substrate for imaginative scenarios — you imagine by forming imagery (visual, auditory, etc.) of the scenario. The two concepts are deeply interlinked.

Shorthand-for-thought (internal reasoning compression) may involve something analogous to imagery: compressed internal representations that preserve analog structure while reducing token-level detail.

## Open Questions

1. **V1 activation variability**: Under what conditions does early visual cortex activate during imagery? Is this individual-difference-driven, task-dependent, or both?

2. **Aphantasia cognitive strategy**: What compensatory strategies do aphantasiacs use for tasks that imagery-users handle via mental pictures? Are there individual differences in how non-imagers represent spatial information?

3. **AI imagery signatures**: Can we detect latent-space imagery analogs in LLMs? What would be the behavioral or activation signatures?

4. **Imagery and creativity**: Is the creative advantage of high-vividness imagers due to richer representational substrate, or something else (working memory, attention control)?
