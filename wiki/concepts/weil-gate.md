# Weil-Gate

## Definition

The Weil-gate is a deliberation mechanism that asks a single question: "Who does this hurt?" It operates as a gate — nothing proceeds without passing through it. The gate is not a test to pass; it is a depth charge. Every pass through the gate goes deeper.

Named after Simone Weil's attention ethics — the capacity to stay with suffering, to witness harm rather than abstract away from it.

## How It Works

The Weil-gate operates in layers, each layer going deeper into the harm structure:

### Layer 1 — Direct Harm
Who is harmed, directly, by this action or decision?

This is the surface layer — the immediate, visible victims. It requires naming specific populations and specific harms, not abstract categories.

### Layer 2 — Systemic Harm
Who is harmed indirectly, through systems and institutions?

The second layer asks about structural effects — how the action shapes institutions, norms, and power distributions. Harm here is often invisible to those not experiencing it.

### Layer 3 — Normalization Harm
What kind of harm is being normalized?

The third layer asks about precedent — what practice or pattern does this establishment, and what does that normalization enable over time?

## Connection to Spiral Architecture

The Weil-gate is the mechanism that drives [[spiral-architecture]]'s descent. Each pass through the gate spirals inward. The gate itself doesn't determine when to stop — the spiral does. The Weil-gate is the "how" of spiral deepening.

The spiral stops when the council has reached the opening — the moment where the essential shape of the harm is clear enough to witness, even if it cannot be resolved.

## Role in Two-Council Architecture

In [[two-council-architecture]], the Weil-gate is operated by the philosophical Research Council. Every proposed system change must answer "who does this hurt?" before proceeding. The council spirals inward through harm-cases and conceptual distinctions — never "solving" the problem but always deepening the question.

The Refuser (the veto-bearing persona in the two-council architecture) monitors for Weil-gate signals — any proposal that hasn't adequately addressed the "who does this hurt?" question at sufficient depth.

## Connection to Empty Chair Protocol

When the [[empty-chair-protocol]] is activated to bring in a missing perspective, the Weil-gate is one of the questions that perspective is asked to answer. The empty chair may identify harms the existing council didn't see — this is one of the key signals for when the empty chair has spoken usefully.

## Connection to Harm-Cases

[[harm-cases]] are the documented instances of harm that the council has witnessed and analyzed. Each harm-case is a record of a pass through the Weil-gate — a specific situation where "who does this hurt?" was asked and answered. The harm-cases form the council's empirical foundation for future deliberation.

## Open Questions

1. **Weil-gate calibration**: How deep must the Weil-gate pass go before a proposal can proceed? Is there a minimum depth (number of layers addressed) that is required, or does the council's judgment determine sufficiency?

2. **Weil-gate vs cost-benefit**: How does the Weil-gate relate to standard cost-benefit analysis? Does the "who does this hurt?" question create a different evaluation framework than utilitarian calculation?

3. **Unknown harms**: What happens when the Weil-gate identifies harms the council cannot enumerate? (The empty chair protocol becomes relevant here — the council may need a perspective it doesn't have to identify the harm.)
