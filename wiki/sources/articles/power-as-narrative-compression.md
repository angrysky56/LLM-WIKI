# Power as Narrative Compression

**Source:** [Medium — @SeloSlav](https://medium.com/@SeloSlav/power-hindsight-counterfactuals-d18946e77689)
**Author:** [[Martin Erlic]]
**Published:** 2025-12-12
**Clipped:** 2026-06-06
**Type:** Source article — systems / economics / political philosophy

## Summary

Power is not a posture. It is a mechanical capacity to move a decision forward while alternative paths remain uncarried — and that capacity reduces to a ratio: **hindsight ÷ counterfactuals**. The article argues that scale raises power not by conferring foresight but by suppressing the visibility of branching histories. Restoring balance requires *institutionalized counterfactuals* — mechanisms that preserve alternative futures long enough to matter.

## The Article (full text)

> **A note on authority and scale**
>
> What does power look like when it operates as a measurable capacity rather than a posture or claim? Power is the ability to move a decision forward while alternative paths remain uncarried and the surrounding system absorbs the resulting load. Action proceeds on accumulated momentum, and unchosen options persist as background pressure rather than active constraint. As justification loses immediacy, explanation shifts its timing and direction. Language turns toward reconstruction, arranging causes around outcomes that already occupy space and continue to shape what follows.
>
> Hindsight is the preferred raw material. After outcomes settle, events line up cleanly. Noise drops out. Accidents fade. Timing looks intentional. The path taken appears singular. Hindsight produces a smooth surface that feels explanatory even when it's merely descriptive.
>
> Counterfactuals disrupt that surface. They reintroduce branching. They force attention to roads not taken, luck, near misses, structural tailwinds, and contingent timing. Counterfactual thinking keeps causal claims honest. It also weakens authority, because authority depends on inevitability.
>
> That relationship can be written simply:
>
> > power = hindsight ÷ counterfactuals
>
> This is not metaphor. It's a mechanical description of how narrative authority behaves under pressure.
>
> As counterfactuals approach zero, power increases rapidly. When alternative explanations are excluded, hindsight expands to fill the gap. The resulting story feels complete. It feels earned. It feels instructive. It also carries less information than it appears to.
>
> People who accumulate authority often retell their own histories this way. Success becomes proof of insight. Failure disappears into irrelevance. Environmental conditions shrink into background texture. The listener receives a clean arc with a protagonist at its center. That arc stabilizes the present position of the speaker. It removes the need to consider what else might have produced the same outcome.
>
> This pattern *scales.*
>
> In small systems, counterfactuals are easy to surface. One person can imagine an alternative decision. A team can replay a missed turn. Feedback arrives quickly. Power remains fragile.
>
> Large systems behave differently. Scale stretches feedback loops. Outcomes lag decisions by years. Attribution becomes murky. Participants lose access to ground truth. Under those conditions, hindsight becomes efficient. It compresses complexity into something manageable. Power rewards that compression.
>
> Economics at scale runs on this dynamic.
>
> Market winners often narrate dominance as foresight. Timing, access to capital, regulatory shelter, network effects, and demand shocks fade into footnotes. What remains is a story of insight, discipline, and vision. That story justifies present concentration. It reassures incumbents. It discourages structural inquiry.
>
> Counterfactual analysis threatens this equilibrium. Asking whether a firm would exist under different interest rates, different trade regimes, different platform rules, or different accident sequences undermines the inevitability narrative. That questioning feels destabilizing. Power responds by minimizing it.
>
> At scale, institutions formalize hindsight. Metrics get selected after outcomes emerge. Models get fit to historical data that already encodes the result. Risk frameworks treat realized paths as representative. Tail events disappear into variance. Learning turns into narrative maintenance.
>
> This does not require malice. It's a selection effect. Systems reward actors who tell convincing stories about why they sit where they sit. Those stories rely on hindsight. They downweight branching histories. Over time, counterfactual capacity erodes.
>
> Economic concentration accelerates the process. When few entities control capital flows, employment, infrastructure, or platforms, alternative futures become harder to test. Entry barriers rise. Experiments shrink. The observed world collapses toward what already exists. Hindsight strengthens because fewer competing outcomes survive long enough to be remembered.
>
> This helps explain why large-scale economic debates often feel shallow. Participants argue over moral framing while skipping structural contingency. Policy discussions reference past success as proof of correctness. Failure gets attributed to execution errors rather than underlying conditions. Power speaks fluently about history while remaining vague about possibility.
>
> Reintroducing counterfactuals restores signal. It forces acknowledgment of luck, timing, and external scaffolding. It complicates authority. It also improves decision quality. Engineering disciplines understand this intuitively. Postmortems focus on alternative failure modes. Safety depends on imagining what almost happened.
>
> Economics struggles with that mindset at scale because power resists it. Admitting counterfactuals dilutes narrative control. It invites redistribution of credit. It weakens claims of inevitability. That resistance shows up as confidence, certainty, and overfitting to the past.
>
> The equation holds because division matters. Hindsight divided by a large counterfactual set yields modest power. Hindsight divided by a tiny counterfactual set yields dominance. Remove alternatives and stories harden into doctrine.
>
> This framing avoids moral language. It does not accuse individuals of stupidity. It describes incentives. Powerholders do not need to entertain branching worlds. Others do. That asymmetry shapes discourse, policy, and markets.
>
> Understanding this helps clarify why economic systems drift toward technocratic certainty while growing more fragile. Hindsight remains abundant. Counterfactual capacity declines. Power consolidates. When shocks arrive, surprise follows.
>
> Restoring balance requires institutionalized counterfactuals. Scenario analysis with teeth. Structural humility. Mechanisms that preserve alternative futures long enough to matter. Without that, hindsight continues to inflate authority until reality intervenes.
>
> The equation is simple because the mechanism is simple. Power feeds on compressed history. Counterfactuals dilute it. Scale decides which side wins.
>

## Key Concepts (extracted from the source)

### The equation

`power = hindsight ÷ counterfactuals`

Two variables, no constants. The framing deliberately drops moral language: power is not bad actors; it is what happens mechanically when hindsight outruns branching history. The article emphasizes this is **not metaphor** — it is a description of how narrative authority behaves under pressure.

### Hindsight

The "preferred raw material" of power. After outcomes settle, hindsight does three things:
1. Strips noise and accidents
2. Makes timing look intentional
3. Produces a smooth surface that *feels* explanatory (often when it is merely descriptive)

Hindsight is *efficient* under large systems because the system itself has stretched feedback loops to the point where the ground truth is no longer accessible to most participants. Compression becomes a survival skill for narrators.

### Counterfactuals

The counter-weight. Counterfactual thinking is what "keeps causal claims honest" — it reintroduces branching, near misses, luck, structural tailwinds, and contingent timing. It is also what authority *cannot afford*, because authority depends on inevitability. The article notes:

> *Counterfactuals dilute power. The power equation holds because division matters.*

### The mechanism of scale

The article's most interesting move is treating scale as the variable that *tilts* the ratio. The mechanism runs:

- **Small systems**: feedback loops are tight. Counterfactuals are easy to surface. Power is fragile.
- **Large systems**: feedback loops stretch. Outcomes lag decisions by years. Attribution becomes murky. Ground truth is lost.
- **Concentrated systems**: counterfactual testing itself becomes hard. "The observed world collapses toward what already exists."

So scale is not neutral. Scale *asymmetrically* favors power, because it shrinks the denominator (counterfactuals) while leaving the numerator (hindsight) abundant.

### Selection effect, not malice

A central rhetorical move: "This does not require malice. It's a selection effect." The article explicitly refuses to moralize. Systems reward actors who tell convincing stories. Over time, counterfactual capacity erodes *as a property of the system*, not as a conspiracy of bad actors.

This matters because the implied intervention is also structural, not moral: you don't fix the system by replacing bad actors; you fix it by **institutionalizing counterfactuals**.

### Institutionalized counterfactuals

The article's prescriptive note: "Scenario analysis with teeth. Structural humility. Mechanisms that preserve alternative futures long enough to matter."

This is the engineering-discipline insight: postmortems in safety-critical engineering work precisely because they *force* the team to imagine alternative failure modes. Economics, by contrast, treats realized paths as representative — and is structurally resistant to the practice because admitting counterfactuals "dilutes narrative control, invites redistribution of credit, weakens claims of inevitability."

## Threads to Pull (curator's notes)

The article is short, almost aphoristic, but the ratio is load-bearing. Several threads worth pulling on:

### 1. Information theory of power

`power = hindsight / counterfactuals` reads like an information ratio. Hindsight is *signal*; counterfactuals are *degrees of freedom*. The ratio is large when the system has collapsed its option space. This rhymes with [[concepts/compression]] — a compressed representation is one where the *effective* degrees of freedom have shrunk even if the raw code space has not. Power and compression are the same operation seen from different angles.

### 2. Power-law scaling

The article's claim that "scale stretches feedback loops" is the same dynamic that produces [[concepts/power-law-scaling]]: in many real systems, a small number of nodes accumulate disproportionate weight, and the rest of the system adapts by reference rather than by experiment. The counterfactual surface shrinks because the "what if we did it differently?" question becomes economically unviable to ask.

### 3. Epistemic energy and feedback

The article describes a feedback loop where reduced counterfactual capacity leads to less learning, which leads to more confidence in hindsight, which leads to *more* dominance, which closes the loop. This is structurally similar to a [[concepts/feedback-activity]] model where positive feedback around authority produces fragility. The article explicitly notes: "Economic systems drift toward technocratic certainty while growing more fragile. ... When shocks arrive, surprise follows."

### 4. Authority and inevitability

"Authority depends on inevitability." This is a strong claim. It implies that the *defense* of authority is structurally a *defense of the singular path* — the work done to make alternatives look impossible or unimportant. Counterfactual analysis is destabilizing not because it adds new facts but because it re-opens *which facts matter*.

### 5. Engineering vs. economics epistemics

The article draws a sharp line: engineering disciplines (postmortems, safety analysis, red-teaming) *use* counterfactuals as a discipline; economics at scale *resists* them because they threaten narrative control. This is an epistemological claim about which fields can learn and which can't.

### 6. Asymmetric incentive structures

"Powerholders do not need to entertain branching worlds. Others do." This is a description of *asymmetric epistemic cost*. The cost of failing to consider counterfactuals falls on the powerless; the cost of fabricating inevitability falls on nobody. The article is essentially describing a **moral hazard** at the level of discourse, not finance.

### 7. The Babushka saga as praxis

The author (Erlic) is a *novelist* as well as a systems thinker, and the article's flat, declarative prose has the rhythm of someone who writes long-form fiction about Eastern European history. The Babushka saga is a speculative fiction shaped by that history — i.e. a long exercise in counterfactual storytelling about a region that has been the subject of enormous hindsight-narration by outsiders. There may be a research thread here: fiction as a deliberate counterfactual practice.
